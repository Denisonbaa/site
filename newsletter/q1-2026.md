      The Black Student Union is an organization, but more than anything it is a
      home. Since 1969, BSU has empowered Black students and the greater Denison
      Community through nourishment; whether that be through catered meals or
      curated education. It has been a necessary voice and force for Black
      students, faculty, and staff; pushing for change, equity, and
      justice.  Our beloved organization has been a physical and metaphorical
      shelter, where Blackness is celebrated, authenticity cherished, and
      individuality embraced. 


      Due to recent administrative choices, BSU’s space, sign, and general activity have been embattled. While we can understand strategic change, we cannot and will not stray from our organization’s founding purpose, values, and ideals. Now more than ever, action necessitates radical unity, and love. During my term my hope is to unite, educate, and remember. Through intentional and educational programming I hope that BSU is able to unite all Black students across campus, as well as other C3 organizations. Finally, I want us to remember; to live by the principle of Sankofa. 


      The Adinkra symbol Sankofa of the Akan people means “it is not foolish to go back for that which you have forgotten” or in short “Go back and get it!” I want us to remember our roots and to learn from the past so that we can be intentional in education and the reclamation of our future. I want to keep BSU’s traditions while elevating our impact! 


      The most important and helpful thing that alumni can do is to connect with us and support us! We need your voices, leverage, and wisdom to be able to accomplish the mission of BSU in its entirety. We value all the guidance, stories, opportunities, and love you all give to us and look forward to further solidifying alumni and student relationships! Three specific and actionable items you can do to stay connected and involved are:


      * Advocate for Black students and BSU to Denison administration

      * Connect with us on social media or visit us in person! 

      * Continue to build a strong alumni network!

      * Contribute to the BSU emergency/reserve fund 


      We look forward to a year filled with unity, love, and education.


      Best, 


      Chloe Echols

      Some gifts arrive with a melody already written into them. Tehillah was a
      student a cappella gospel group at Denison — voices coming together across
      dorm rooms and rehearsal halls to sing achingly beautiful gospel in a way
      that was undeniable: as a shared testimony, built on more than one part.
      The choir crossed lines the way gospel music always has — Black and white
      students, holding different notes, making one sound together.


      Tehillah is a Hebrew word which means “to sing a hymn of spontaneous praise unto the Lord”. These singers wanted to expose Denison community to a positive and inspirational message through  gospel music and spread the gospel through praise and worship. Tehillah's offering was never only sound, either. Over their years together, the group quietly raised money — show by show, a little added here and there — and when the final class of Tehillah singers graduated in May 2023, they made one more decision before the group closed its songbook for the last time: to gift whatever was left toward supporting the BSU and Black student education at Denison.


      That decision sat waiting for three years, until one of those graduating singers, Jen Clancey '23, reached out to the BAA to carry it through. She was one of the voices that made Tehillah's harmony what it was, and now is fulfilling that commitment of the graduating seniors of the Tehillah Gospel Choir, leaving a legacy of support for Black students at Denison.


      It's a small story, but it's the kind worth telling. A gospel choir doesn't just sing in unison — it sings in harmony, different voices holding their own part so the whole can rise higher than any one of them could alone. Tehillah's final gift works the same way: one voice, years later, still carrying the whole choir's intention forward. Their music is a testimony and thankfully for us, their voices are not lost in time, but captured for all time for us to hear.

    body: >
      My youngest nephew graduated from high school this Spring. It was a joyous
      occasion, but the 6 months prior was anything but as he scrambled to find
      scholarships which would put  his college choices within reach. The
      experience highlighted the reality and fall-out of the end of DEI. Quite
      frankly, the whole college funding game has completely changed for
      minority students. Since the Supreme Court killed affirmative action, a
      massive wave of colleges panicked and started quietly axing diversity
      scholarships too—even though the court's ruling didn't actually mention
      financial aid. Here’s a brief look at the changes:


      The Shrinking pool of DEI based Awards

      Colleges are changing their rules to avoid getting sued by conservative legal groups. The numbers show just how fast the door is closing: 


      The Drop: The National Scholarship Providers Association found that the share of scholarships looking at race or gender dropped from 15% down to just 11% in mid-2024.


      The Pivot: Big-name public flagship schools have already wiped out or altered at least $60 million in race-conscious aid.


      The Rebrand: Instead of targeting specific minority groups, schools are flipping these funds into strictly "need-based" or "first-generation" cash. For example, the University of Iowa completely shifted its $9.4 million Advantage Iowa Award away from underrepresented racial groups.  Even private donors are changing their criteria. The Gates Foundation officially dropped all racial and ethnic eligibility requirements from its signature scholarship program and, instead, opened the fund to all Pell Grant-eligible students regardless of race.


      The Takeaway

      Where you apply matters more than ever. Each of my nephew’s top choices eliminated DEI and race-based scholarships. However, a 2024 survey of 50 universities showed 13 completely changed or cut their race-conscious scholarships, while 22 decided to keep them.  For example, the University of Alabama ended its National Recognition Scholarship, a full-ride scholarship for high-performing Black, Latino, and Indigenous students. On the other hand, Penn State kept its Educational Equity scholarships and race-based departmental awards,  arguing that the court only banned race-conscious admissions, not financial aid. Relying on the same reasoning, the University of New Mexico kept its DEI based National Recognition award.


      At the last minute, it worked out for my nephew.  He was awarded a scholarship that covers the $13,000 gap between attending and not attending college. But my nephew was lucky. With an increasing number of schools voluntarily ending race-based financial aid, luck for minority students in the Class of 2027  may just run out. 


      Felice Harris '89 - Historian

## BAA Endowment Update

**Background:** The Supreme Court's [Harvard v. SFA ](https://www.harvard.edu/admissionscase/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2022/05/FINAL_Key-Facts_FINAL.pdf)decision prohibited the use of race in college admissions. Higher education uniformly interprets that case to also prohibit the administration of scholarships on the basis of racial identity. Prior to the Trump administration coming into office, colleges and universities had continued to administer such scholarships to one degree or another.  The Trump administration adopted an aggressive enforcement posture and issued guidance from the Dept. of Justice stating their interpretation that identity based scholarships are unlawful.

**Current Status:**  Denison has asked the BAA to consent to an amendment of the gift agreement which governs the BAA Endowment to change it from an identity based award to a need based award. It would become a generic scholarship award no longer set aside for Black students, contrary to the BAA's intent when the scholarship was created.  The BAA has refused consent to such an amendment and will not now nor in the future consent to any change that will diminish the Black serving purpose of the BAA endowment.  Based on our review of the legal information available, the BAA's preferred resolution is transfer of the endowment to a new steward that is not prohibited from administering the endowment according to its original donor intent.  Based on preliminary legal research,  the BAA concludes that there is a viable legal pathway for transfer of the endowment to a new steward.  To date, Denison's response has been that they do not believe such a transfer is lawful. Denison has not provided the BAA with any legal analysis or legal opinion to support this assertion. 

**Going Forward:** The BAA will seek a legal opinion to clarify the legal risks and issues involved with the transfer of a charitable trust and continue negotiations with Denison based on the results of that research.  The BAA has issued requests for support to the legal clinics at Howard University, Ohio State College of Law and the University of Cincinnati. The issues involved are complex and we expect that this negotiation process will take at least a year to resolve. During this period, administration of scholarships from the endowment are suspended and will remain so until a resolution that preserves donor intent has been obtained. Suspension of the scholarship is not satisfactory to the BAA or to Denison. However, the resolution of the endowment is a generational decision, which cannot be undone once made. The BAA is committed to getting that resolution right for the generations to come.

## BAA Capital Collective - The Future of Black Alumni Philanthropy

The BAA is preparing to launch the next generation of alumni giving: the **BAA Capital Collective**.

The Capital Collective will be the primary way Black alumni contribute to the BAA’s annual operating budget, which is currently approximately $12,000 per year. That budget supports the core work of the Association, including student programs, communications, alumni engagement, financial administration, governance, and other priorities approved by the Executive Board.

The idea is simple: instead of relying mainly on occasional fundraising appeals, alumni will be able to make recurring or one-time contributions into a shared pool of resources. Small and moderate gifts, combined across the membership, can provide the BAA with a more predictable and sustainable financial base.

The Collective will also support strategic priorities beyond the annual budget, including work related to the BAA Endowment, Black student life, organizational infrastructure, and other long-term initiatives.

The Capital Collective is **not active *yet***. Denison currently serves as the BAA’s fiscal sponsor. Before launching the Collective, the BAA must establish a new fiscal-sponsorship infrastructure that provides clearer financial separation, reliable reporting, and full BAA control over how funds are managed and used. Our current fiscal sponsorship arrangement with Denison has served us well for many years, but is not sufficient to the task before us now.  The BAA has made application to [the Social Good Fund ](https://www.socialgoodfund.org/fiscal-sponsorship/about-our-sponsorship/)for fiscal sponsorship and our application is pending. Once that structure is in place, members will receive more information about contribution options, financial reporting, funding priorities, and the official launch.

The goal is not simply to create another fundraising campaign. It is to build a dependable way for Black alumni to finance the BAA’s work, strengthen its independence, and turn collective support into lasting capacity.
